Coastal dune lakes of Walton County

Rare, imperiled lakes along about 26 miles of Gulf coast. Most of the time sand keeps the sea out. When an outfall opens, the water turns brackish, then begins to freshen again.

One of Florida’s most unusual natural systems

Walton County’s coastal dune lakes sit just behind the Gulf’s dunes. Unlike ordinary freshwater lakes, their waters can periodically connect with the Gulf through naturally opening and closing outfalls. That exchange shapes the lakes, their wildlife and the communities around them.

How these lakes work

Rare, and gathered on one coast

A basin of fresh to brackish water, held in coastal dunes and opening to the sea only now and then, is an uncommon kind of lake, and an imperiled one. Walton County’s stretch of the Florida Panhandle is unusual because the lakes are not scattered one-offs. About 15 named lakes lie along roughly 26 miles of shoreline.

The water is often tea- or black-colored from dissolved organic matter washed in from the watershed. Rain, groundwater, and occasional Gulf surges all feed the basins, so chemistry and salinity can shift from lake to lake and from month to month.

They share a setting, not a single size or a single owner. Campbell Lake is the clearest intact case in this set: watershed, lake, and outfall all sit in protected state land at Topsail. Stallworth Lake is about 15 acres, with a largely private shore. Lake Powell, on the line with Bay County, is the giant of the chain. Choctawhatchee Basin Alliance morphometry puts it near 710 acres. Some park descriptions say nearly 800. The entire lake is an Outstanding Florida Water.

Fresh, then brackish

Rain and shallow groundwater fill the basins. A berm of beach sand usually stands between the lake and the Gulf of America. When the lake is high enough, or a storm cuts the beach, the berm can fail. Water runs out. Gulf water runs in. Salinity rises, and the lake becomes brackish. Marine life can use the channel. Sand later closes the cut, and freshwater inflows begin to freshen the lake again. The exchange is intermittent. These are not ordinary tidal bays, and they are not ponds that never taste salt.

Fuller Lake does not fit the outfall pattern. It has no direct opening of its own. It is hydrologically connected with Morris Lake, the next lake east, in the dunes at Topsail. Allen Lake shows a different human arrangement: it sits in view of County Road 30A, and its drainage to the Gulf passes under the road. Western Lake, about 244 acres and several lobes wide, is where most people can watch an outfall open and close.

Watched in partnership

Walton County partners with the Choctawhatchee Basin Alliance to watch level, salinity, and whether an outfall is open. Those readings move with rain, groundwater, and storms, so one afternoon at the shore is a poor summary of a lake’s year. In 2002 the County also created the Coastal Dune Lakes Advisory Board to help guide protection as the coast grew. A 2008 Florida LAKEWATCH report for that board still underlines a simple need: clear, ongoing information for residents and visitors as shoreline ownership changes.

The acreages in this guide are approximate for the same reason published numbers disagree. Studies trace the shoreline differently, and a lake does not hold one permanent outline. Where a second figure is widely repeated, the lake page includes it.
